The Price of Access: Biometrics and Financial Inclusion

Dario Benes and team Fine Print Partners from the FGCU Business Ethics Society presented The Price of Access to the board of Access Now, a nonprofit digital rights organization, at the IBESCC national business ethics competition. The case asked whether biometric identity systems expand access to finance or create permanent risks for the people they were built to include.

The problem

Unlike a password, a fingerprint cannot be reset

Biometric identity systems promised inclusion for people without documents. When a database leaks, the promise inverts: theft is permanent, there is no bureau or lawyer to call, and victims living on $2 to $4 a day can be left legally liable for fraudulent debt or lose redirected government benefits.

The team framed the tension as UN Sustainable Development Goal 10 (reduce inequality) against Goal 16 (accountable, inclusive institutions). Biometric financial inclusion could unlock about $3.7 trillion in GDP (McKinsey), while 22 African nations have no data protection legislation.

Cases examined included Worldcoin’s iris scans for cryptocurrency in Kenya, suspended by the government and later stripped of its registration by Kenya’s data protection office; the Mastercard and GAVI “Wellness Pass” in West Africa; and Santoshi Kumari, an 11-year-old in Jharkhand, India, who died in 2017 after a digital database failed to recognize her family.

Recommendation

Infrastructure and oversight

  • Privacy-preserving infrastructure: cryptographic verification such as zero-knowledge proofs, user-controlled self-sovereign identity, and cancelable biometrics that store a transformed template instead of the raw biometric
  • Transparency: disclose how biometric data is collected, used, stored and shared, in clear language
  • Consent: explicit opt-in, no coercive incentives, and an equivalent non-biometric alternative
  • Data minimization: collect only what is necessary, with defined retention periods and secure deletion aligned with GDPR
  • Accountability: independent audits, enforceable standards and penalties
  • A timeline for Access Now: audit and awareness through the #WHYID campaign (0 to 3 months), testing privacy infrastructure and releasing technical frameworks (3 to 9 months), and a push for mandatory global standards (9 to 18 months)

Ethics

Fairness, respect and duty of care

The harm was foreseeable, and good intent is not a defense. The recommendation keeps the inclusion benefit while shifting risk to the organizations that collect and profit from the data, and treats the harm as what it is: permanent.

Privacy-preserving technology is a software layer with near-zero marginal cost per user. Prevention is cheaper than the penalty.

Takeaways

Key takeaways

  • Inclusion and rights can conflict, and the people with the fewest resources carry the risk
  • Privacy-preserving infrastructure is ethically sound and financially rational
  • Accountability has to be structural, not aspirational
