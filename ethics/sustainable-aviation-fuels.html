Sustainable Aviation Fuels: The Gap Between a 10% Promise and the Supply

Dario Benes, with Ashley Fearday and Emalee Postma of the FGCU Business Ethics Society, presented a case on sustainable aviation fuel (SAF) at the Stetson national business ethics competition. The case weighed Delta Air Lines’ goal of replacing 10% of its fossil jet fuel with SAF by the end of 2030 against the supply that exists to meet it.

The problem

Aviation is the hardest transport sector to decarbonize

Aviation is responsible for about 2.5% of global CO₂ emissions, would rank as the 11th largest emitter if it were a country, and has the fastest emissions growth of rail, road and shipping. About 4% of global temperature increase is attributed to aviation fuels.

SAF is the main near-term lever, but it is constrained on every side: a cost premium over jet fuel, scarce feedstock, and a 50% blend limit. Current production is about 300 million liters against future demand of about 450 billion liters.

Legal environment

Binding in Europe, voluntary in the U.S.

  • European Union: ReFuelEU Aviation sets a mandatory SAF blend for suppliers (6% by 2030, 20% by 2035, 70% by 2050), and the Emissions Trading System makes airlines pay for CO₂
  • United States: the Airline Deregulation Act of 1978 blocks state-level SAF requirements; incentives come through federal SAF tax credits, alongside deception claims over sustainability marketing
  • International: ICAO’s goal of net-zero international aviation emissions by 2050, and CORSIA carbon offsets on international routes

Options assessed

SAF is necessary, but not sufficient

  • Lower carbon aviation fuel (LCAF)
  • Scimitar winglets: 3% to 5% fuel savings, with long-term fuel savings and a positive ROIC in the team’s analysis
  • NEO-series fleet renewal: up to 20% less fuel burn
  • Sustainable aviation fuel, within EU blend mandates
  • Hybrid-electric aircraft: 30% to 40% CO₂ reduction
  • Airbus hydrogen partnership: 100% CO₂ reduction

Ethics

Deontology and stakeholder theory

A duty-based reading tests the airline’s public promises, a 10% SAF target by 2030 and net zero by 2050, against what can actually be delivered. Stakeholder theory weighs employees (job stability and upskilling), shareholders (disciplined capital allocation), communities, customers, SAF producers and regulators.

Takeaways

Key takeaways

  • SAF is necessary, but not sufficient
  • A phased strategy is most effective
  • Proven efficiency investments such as winglets earn a positive return while SAF supply scales
