The Hermosa Project: South32’s Social License to Operate

Dario Benes and Nicholas Colbert, team Stop and Zinc from Florida Gulf Coast University, advised South32’s board on its Hermosa mine at the 2026 Collegiate Ethics Case Competition, hosted by the Center for Leadership Ethics at the University of Arizona. Their recommendation: continue Hermosa with four binding commitments costing US$203M in present value. FGCU later asked Dario to present the case to the Comptroller during his campus visit.

The problem

Permitted is not accepted

Hermosa is a US$3.3 billion zinc, lead, silver and battery-grade manganese project in the Patagonia Mountains of Santa Cruz County, Arizona. It was about 50% built, with first ore due in 2028, and was the first mining project under FAST-41 federal permitting.

The budget rose 53%, from US$2.16 billion to US$3.3 billion, and first production slipped about a year. On September 29, 2026, CBD and PARA sued the Forest Service (CBD and PARA v. Forest Service).

Neighbors are asked to trust water data that South32 measures, reports and checks itself. The benefits flow to Arizona and global shareholders; the water and wildlife risk stays in Santa Cruz County.

Recommendation

Four binding commitments

  • Independent water monitoring: quarterly sampling of groundwater, mine discharge, Harshaw Creek and neighbor wells by a lab the community picks and South32 pays for, with plain-language public results and an immediate stop-and-fix rule if a limit is crossed.
  • A binding Good Neighbor Agreement with the Town of Patagonia, Santa Cruz County, 12 consulted Tribes and nearby well owners: any damaged well replaced, local hiring in writing, trucks kept out of town, and an equal-seat oversight committee.
  • A community benefit fund at 2% of EBITDA, about US$12.5M a year from first production, community-run with South32 holding one seat.
  • A legacy endowment that saves one third of the fund, about US$4.2M a year, grows to about US$260M by closure, and pays about US$10M a year after the mine closes.

Ethical frameworks

Kantian duty and stakeholder theory

Kant’s principle of humanity, to treat people as ends and never merely as means, tests South32’s public promises: self-reported water data, targets that are not contracts, and consultation without a vote.

R. Edward Freeman’s stakeholder theory weighs the community, Tribes, employees, shareholders, regulators and opponents. In the team model, about 45% of revenue goes to shareholders, mostly outside Arizona, while the plan asks about 1% for the community.

Financials

Ethics costs less than delay

South32 values Hermosa at US$3.1 billion (post-tax NPV at 7% real), with a 19% IRR on the money left to spend and about US$650M of EBITDA a year at full capacity.

The full plan costs US$203M in present value: 6.5% of South32’s own valuation and about 2% of a year’s EBITDA. Each month of delay costs about US$26M, so avoiding about eight months of delay pays for the whole plan.

Precedents

Proven in mining and resource wealth

  • Stillwater, Montana (2000): a binding Good Neighbor Agreement, with 24+ years and no environmental lawsuit
  • Ekati, Canada (1997): an independent monitoring agency whose directors Indigenous groups appoint
  • Red Dog, Alaska: pays NANA 40% of net proceeds, US$327M in 2024
  • Alaska Permanent Fund and Norway’s oil fund: the models for the endowment and its spending rule
  • Jerome, Arizona: more than 10,000 residents in the 1920s and fewer than 100 after its mine closed in 1953, with no fund

Takeaways

Key takeaways

  • Permitted is not accepted: federal approvals are in hand, neighbors’ trust is not
  • Verify, sign, share
  • Ethics costs less than delay
